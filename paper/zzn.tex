\title{Two-Colour Zig-Zag Numberlink on Rectangles:\\
A Forbidden-Pattern Characterization and a Polynomial-Time\\
Construction, Verified in Lean}
\author{Z. Zzyzek\thanks{To the extent possible under law, the author has waived all copyright and

CC0 1.0 Universal (\url{https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/}).}}
\date{Draft, November 2026}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In Zig-Zag Numberlink, pairs of endpoint cells in a grid must be joined by vertex-disjoint paths
that together visit every cell. This paper solves the case of two pairs on a rectangular grid, with
the endpoints anywhere in the grid. When a side is at most $10$, a plug dynamic programming
algorithm decides the instance. When both sides are at least $11$, an instance is solvable if and
only if no entry of a finite catalogue of forbidden patterns matches it. On a $w\times h$ grid
the decision takes $O(wh)$ counted steps, and a construction returns a solution of every solvable
instance within $O(M^2)$ counted steps, where $M=\max(w,h)$. Each pattern is shown to
rule out solutions by a parity count, planarity arguments, local arguments, or an enumeration of
how two paths can cover a square window at a corner. For the converse, the proof deletes pairs of
adjacent lines that hold no endpoint, under conditions that keep an instance free of matching
patterns, inserts the lines back into solutions, and settles the remaining grids,
with both sides between $11$ and $16$, by machine-found certificates. The characterization, the
decision procedure, the construction and the step bounds are proved in Lean~4, with the
certificates and enumerations checked by the Lean kernel.
\end{abstract}

An instance of \emph{two-colour Zig-Zag Numberlink}, the case of two pairs of the puzzle studied by
Adcock et al.~\cite{adcock15}, is a rectangular grid with four endpoint cells, $s_0,t_0$ of colour~$0$ and
$s_1,t_1$ of colour~$1$. A solution is a path from $s_0$ to $t_0$ and a path from $s_1$ to $t_1$
that share no cell and together visit every cell (Figure~\ref{fig:problem}(a) and~(b)).

In graph terms this is the paired many-to-many $2$-disjoint path cover problem on a rectangular
grid graph~\cite{kk24}. This paper decides, for two pairs with the endpoints anywhere in the grid,
whether a solution exists, and constructs one when it does.

With one pair the problem asks for a Hamiltonian path, a path through every cell, between two
given cells. Give the cells the signs $+1$ and $-1$ in the pattern of a chessboard. Along a path
the signs alternate, which constrains the signs of the two ends. For two distinct cells of a
rectangle with both sides at least $4$, this \emph{sign condition} alone decides whether a
Hamiltonian path joins them~\cite{ips82}.

% lean: ZZN/Parity.lean:112 parity_of_solvable
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:538 t1_sound
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:360 testL2_sound
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:634 edgeClosure_sound
With two pairs the sign condition becomes a condition on the four endpoints, the \emph{parity
condition}, which every solvable instance of every size satisfies. It is not sufficient, because
the two paths block each other. At the border, two pairs whose colours alternate around the border
cannot both be joined without a crossing (Figure~\ref{fig:intro}(c)). At a corner, an empty
corner cell between endpoints of different colours can be covered by neither path
(Figure~\ref{fig:intro}(d)). Along an edge, endpoints of the two colours can force both paths to
close after three cells each, which leaves the rest of the grid uncovered. A characterization has
to capture these interactions for grids of every size, while a computer check covers only finitely
many.

% lean: FinalN.lean:43 passes_iff_solvable_K
This paper gives a finite \emph{catalogue} of forbidden patterns, which includes the three above,
and proves that an instance with both sides at least $11$ is solvable exactly when no pattern
matches it. That each pattern rules out solutions is shown by parity, planarity and local
arguments, together with enumerations of how two paths can cover a window at a corner.
Sufficiency is shown by deleting pairs of adjacent lines that hold no endpoint, inserting them
back into solutions, and settling the remaining finite set of grids by certificates. The results,
each marked as new or known, are the following.

\secmark{defs}
\subsection{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:25 Coord
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:29 InBounds
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:33 Adjacent
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:52 IsPath
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:41 chainAdjacent
A \emph{cell} is a pair $(x,y)$ of natural numbers, and a $w\times h$ \emph{grid} has the cells
with $x<w$ and $y<h$. The figures draw $x$ as the row index, increasing downward, and $y$ as the
column index, increasing to the right, so a $w\times h$ grid is drawn with $w$ rows and $h$
columns. Two cells are \emph{adjacent} when they differ by $1$ in one coordinate and agree in the
other. A \emph{path} from $s$ to $t$ is a list of distinct cells of the grid that starts at $s$,
ends at $t$, and has adjacent consecutive cells. A \emph{chain} is a list of cells with adjacent
consecutive cells, in which cells may repeat.

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:59 Inst
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:378 fires3 (pts gives s0, t0 colour 0 and s1, t1 colour 1)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:67 Inst.WellFormed
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:74 IsSolution
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:80 Solvable
An \emph{instance} $I$ consists of $w$, $h$ and four cells, the \emph{endpoints}, $s_0,t_0$ of
\emph{colour}~$0$ and $s_1,t_1$ of colour~$1$. It is \emph{well-formed} when the endpoints are
four distinct cells of the grid. A \emph{solution} is a pair of paths, $p$ from $s_0$ to $t_0$ and
$q$ from $s_1$ to $t_1$, with no common cell, such that every cell of the grid lies on $p$ or on
$q$. The instance is \emph{solvable} when it has a solution.
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:141 Nb
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:208 end_unique
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:266 end_exists
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:249 interior
% lean: ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:286 le_two
A \emph{path neighbour} of a cell is a cell directly before or after it on its path. In a solution,
every cell of the grid that is no endpoint has exactly two path neighbours. In a solution of a
well-formed instance an endpoint has exactly one.

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:496 freeLineB
% lean: ZZN/Reductions.lean:30 Inst.FreeLine
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:459 Inst.deleteAt
% lean: ZZN/BandExtension.lean:32 Inst.insertAt
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:484 Inst.transpose
The \emph{line} $x=k$ is the set of cells with first coordinate $k$, drawn as row $k$, and the line
$y=k$ the set with second coordinate $k$, drawn as column $k$. A line is \emph{free} when it holds
no endpoint. \emph{Deleting} two free lines $x=d$ and $x=d+1$ gives the instance with $w-2$ in place of
$w$ in which every endpoint with $x\ge d+2$ has its $x$-coordinate decreased by $2$.
\emph{Inserting} two lines after the line $x=r$ gives the instance with $w+2$ in place of $w$ in
which every endpoint with $x>r$ has its $x$-coordinate increased by $2$. The \emph{transpose} of an
instance exchanges the two coordinates of every cell and exchanges $w$ and $h$. Every operation on
the $x$-axis has a version on the $y$-axis, applied to the transpose.

% lean: ZZN/Skeleton.lean:37 InDom
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:153 sgn
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:154 parityOk
An instance is \emph{wide} when it is well-formed and both sides are at least $11$, and
\emph{thin} when a side is at most $10$. The \emph{sign} of a cell $v=(x,y)$ is $\sigma(v)=+1$ if
$x+y$ is even and $\sigma(v)=-1$ otherwise. An instance satisfies the \emph{parity condition} when
the signs of its four endpoints sum to $2\cdot(wh\bmod 2)$.

% lean: GridHam/Coloring.lean:177 ColorCompatible
% lean: GridHam/Forbidden.lean:77 IsForbidden
% lean: GridHam/Forbidden.lean:85 IsAcceptable
% lean: GridHam/Main.lean:171 ips_characterization
% lean: ZZN/Cut/Tree.lean:117 leafOK (subtracts the corner of the rectangle before the test)
% checked against primary text, 2026-11-19 (ips82, Section 3.1 and Theorem 3.2)
Two distinct cells $s,t$ of a rectangle are an \emph{IPS-admissible} pair, after Itai,
Papadimitriou and Szwarcfiter~\cite{ips82}, when the rectangle has a Hamiltonian path from $s$ to
$t$. These pairs are the ones that satisfy the sign condition and avoid excluded cases, which
occur only with a side of $1$, $2$ or $3$~\cite{ips82}. The sign condition is
$\sigma(s)=\sigma(t)=\sigma(c)$ for a corner cell $c$ of the rectangle if its area is odd, and
$\sigma(s)\ne\sigma(t)$ if its area is even. The Lean development proves this characterization.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

% checked against primary text, 2026-11-19 (ips82: Theorem 2.1, Corollary 1, Theorem 3.2,

\paragraph{Hamiltonian paths in rectangles.} Itai, Papadimitriou and Szwarcfiter~\cite{ips82}
proved the Hamiltonian path problem NP-complete in general grid graphs and characterized the
IPS-admissible pairs of a rectangle. Their proof of sufficiency removes a strip of two lines that
holds neither endpoint and extends a Hamiltonian path of the rest into it, or splits the rectangle
in two at an edge of the path, down to finitely many small problems, which a case analysis solves.
It gives a construction in time linear in the number of cells. This paper follows the same plan for
two paths, with pair deletion and band extension (Section~\ref{sec:delete}) in the role of the
strips and the grids with both sides from $11$ to $16$ in the role of the small problems. The cut
trees of Section~\ref{sec:suff} also split rectangles, and use the sufficiency direction of the
characterization at every leaf that is not a single cell.

% checked against primary text, 2026-11-19 (adcock15, arXiv v1: Theorem 1 and Section 4; the
%   journal text was not opened)
\paragraph{Numberlink and disjoint paths.} Adcock et al.~\cite{adcock15} proved Zig-Zag Numberlink
NP-complete when the number of pairs is part of the input, and asked whether it can be solved in
polynomial time for a constant number of pairs greater than one. For two pairs, this paper gives a
decision procedure and a construction with step bounds polynomial in $w$ and $h$.
% checked against primary text, 2026-11-19 (lynch75, Theorem II)
% UNSURE: primary paper not read (rs95)
Dropping the requirement that the paths cover every cell gives the vertex-disjoint paths problem,
which is NP-complete for an unbounded number of pairs in planar graphs~\cite{lynch75} and solvable
in polynomial time for every fixed number of pairs~\cite{rs95}.

% lean: ZZN/Parity.lean:112 parity_of_solvable, ZZN/SoundLocal.lean:249 interior, :599 cover_le:
%   covering clause used.
Of the catalogue entries (Table~\ref{tab:catalogue}), only \ent{T1}, \ent{T2} and \ent{L1} are
proved to rule out solutions without the covering requirement.

% checked against primary text by the coordinator, 2026-11-19 (makino06: Theorem 1.1, Lemmas 3.1
%   and 3.2, Section 4)
\paragraph{Disjoint path covers.} Makino~\cite{makino06} showed that a toroidal grid with both
sides even and at least $4$ has a two-path cover for every choice of four endpoints with two of
each sign. The proof inserts two rows or two columns into a cover. It states without proof that a
solvable instance stays solvable under such an insertion and that every instance arises by
insertions from finitely many instances. Of these it solves the $4\times 4$ torus by a case
analysis and states the solvability of the others without proof. Band extension
(Lemma~\ref{lem:band}) is this insertion after a free line of a rectangle, where solvability also
depends on the positions of the endpoints, so a deletion needs conditions that keep an instance free of matching
patterns (Lemma~\ref{lem:pass}).
% UNSURE: abstract read, body not read (kk24)
Keshavarz-Kohjerdi~\cite{kk24} studied two-path covers of rectangular grids for endpoints on the
outer face, and gave necessary conditions for such covers to exist and a linear-time algorithm to
compute them. This paper places the endpoints anywhere in the rectangle. Its construction takes
$O(\max(w,h)^2)$ counted steps (Section~\ref{sec:algo}), which is not linear in $wh$ when one side
is much longer than the other.

% checked against primary text, 2026-11-19 (chen08 pp. 1-4, oiwiki, jacobsen07 abstract and
%   Sections 1, 2)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:573 stepCell
% lean: ZZN/WinCheck.lean:57 stepCell
\paragraph{Plug dynamic programming.} The algorithm for thin rectangles is a frontier
(transfer-matrix) method, known in competitive programming as the plug DP~\cite{chen08,oiwiki} and
used in statistical physics to count sets of disjoint paths that together visit every vertex of a
square lattice~\cite{jacobsen07}. This paper uses it as the solver for thin instances and, run over
a square window at a corner, as the enumeration behind the window certificates of
Section~\ref{sec:cat}.

\secmark{main}
\section{Two-colour Zig-Zag Numberlink on rectangular grids}\label{sec:main}

This section determines which instances are solvable and how a solution is found.
Well-formed thin instances are decided by a plug DP (Section~\ref{sec:thin}), which gives an
algorithm and no condition. For wide instances, Section~\ref{sec:cat} lists the forbidden patterns,
the entries of a catalogue, and proves for each entry that it rules out solutions. The converse,
that a wide instance matched by no entry, a passing instance, is solvable, takes two steps.
Section~\ref{sec:delete} shows that two adjacent free lines can be deleted, under conditions that
keep a passing instance passing, and inserted back into any solution. Section~\ref{sec:suff}
deletes lines until both sides are at most $16$, and settles the finitely many remaining instances
by kernel-checked certificates. Section~\ref{sec:algo} turns the proof into a decision procedure
and a construction, with step bounds.

Proofs are complete where they are short. Longer ones are sketches, marked as such, whose
remaining case analysis is in the Lean development, and the step bounds are proved only there. The
finite facts are kernel-checked computations, named as such where they are used.
Section~\ref{sec:thin} needs only the definitions of Section~\ref{sec:defs}. It comes first because
Section~\ref{sec:cat} runs the plug DP on corner windows. Short names such as \ent{T1} refer to the
catalogue entries of Table~\ref{tab:catalogue}.

\secmark{thin}
\subsection{Thin rectangles}\label{sec:thin}

% lean: ZZN/Thin/Fast.lean:163 thinBF_iff
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:663 thinBF
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:622 cell
The \emph{plug DP} decides every well-formed instance, of any size (Theorem~\ref{thm:thin}). With
$h\le w$, after transposing if needed, it processes the lines $x=k$ in turn, each from $y=0$ to
$y=h-1$, so the \emph{frontier} between processed and unprocessed cells runs along the short side
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}).

% lean: ZZN/Thin/Inside.lean:432 Piece
% lean: ZZN/Thin/Inside.lean:436 doneMask
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:573 stepCell
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:566 closeP (different colours, a colour completed twice)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:656 noExit (leaving the grid)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:624 stage (the set of states, duplicates removed)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:657 stageF
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:659 insideF (start state)
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:661 dpAcceptF
A \emph{piece} is a maximal run of a solution path within the processed cells that is not the whole
path. A \emph{state} records the two ends of each piece, each an endpoint with its colour or a
\emph{plug} (a crossing of the frontier into an unprocessed cell), and the set of \emph{complete}
colours, those whose whole path is processed. Processing a cell gives it one path neighbour if it
is an endpoint and two otherwise. The plugs of the state fix the path neighbours above and to the
left, and each choice of the others, below and to the right, gives a successor state. A successor
is discarded if it would close a cycle, join endpoints of different colours, complete a colour
twice, or leave the grid. Starting from the state with no piece and no complete colour, the DP keeps the
set of states \emph{reached} after each cell, and \emph{accepts} when the state with no piece and
both colours complete is reached after the last cell.
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:670 Wit
% lean: ZZN/Constr/ThinWit2.lean:26 stageW2
% lean: ZZN/Constr/ThinWit2.lean:38 thinSolveW2
The version with paths also stores with each reached state the cells of its pieces and complete
paths, for one way of reaching it, and returns the two paths of the accepting state. The
supplement, section ``The plug DP encoding'', gives the state coding and the cell step as
pseudocode.

\begin{theorem}[plug DP]\label{thm:thin}
The plug DP accepts a well-formed instance exactly when it is solvable, and the version with paths
then returns a solution. The version with paths returns only solutions on every instance.
\end{theorem}
% lean: ZZN/Thin/Inside.lean:439 Inv
% lean: ZZN/Thin/Fast.lean:93 insideF_complete
% lean: ZZN/Thin/Sound.lean:50 W
% lean: ZZN/Thin/Fast.lean:32 insideF_sound
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
After every cell, the pieces of a solution form a reached state (the \emph{run invariant}).
Conversely a reached state is backed by disjoint runs of adjacent cells covering the processed
cells, with matching ends and a full path per complete colour, so the accepting state gives a
solution.
\end{proof}

% lean: ZZN/Timed/ThinNC.lean:441 fold_NC
% lean: ZZN/Timed/ThinWord.lean:354 word_inj
% lean: ZZN/Timed/ThinWordCount.lean:248 card_fold
% lean: ZZN/Timed/ThinRopeCost.lean:31 Nw
% lean: ZZN/Timed/ThinWord.lean:379 wcnt (Nw b counts at least the bracket words over the letters
%   empty, open, close, times 4; these grow exponentially in b. Arithmetic on the definition, not a
%   Lean theorem.)
The pieces of a reached state do not cross, so at most $N(b)$ states are reached after any cell,
where $b=\min(w,h)$ and $N(b)$ counts bracket words with colour marks, one letter for each of the
$b+1$ places where a piece can cross the frontier, together with the set of complete colours
(supplement, section ``The plug DP encoding''). $N(b)$ grows exponentially in $b$ (a
count on its definition, not a Lean theorem), and so does the bound on the plug DP's steps per
cell (Section~\ref{sec:algo}).

\subsection{The catalogue and why each entry rules out solutions}\label{sec:cat}

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:377 fires3
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:385 Passes
This subsection lists the reasons a wide instance can be unsolvable as the entries of a finite
catalogue, in four groups, global (a sign count and planarity), local, forced-route and window. An
entry \emph{fires} on an instance when its pattern matches, and an instance \emph{passes} the
catalogue when no entry fires. The subsection proves the following theorem, at its end.

% lean: WinKU/Facts.lean:170 theoremB_K
% lean: ZZN/TheoremB.lean:63 theoremB
\begin{theorem}[necessity]\label{thm:sound}
Every solvable wide instance passes the catalogue.
\end{theorem}

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:123 Frame

The \emph{border} is the set of cells on the first and last line of each axis. Its \emph{clockwise
order} starts at $(0,0)$ and runs along $x=0$, then $y=h-1$, then $x=w-1$, then $y=0$. A
\emph{corner frame} is one of the $8$ coordinate systems given by reversing the rows or not,
reversing the columns or not, and then transposing or not. Cell $(x,y)$ has frame cell $(x',y')$,
with $x'=w-1-x$ if the rows are reversed and $x'=x$ otherwise, $y'=h-1-y$ if the columns are
reversed and $y'=y$ otherwise, and then $(y',x')$ in place of $(x',y')$ if the frame is transposed.
Frame cell $(0,0)$ is a corner of the grid. Frame cells are written (row, column), and in the
\emph{identity frame}, with no reversal and no transposition, the row is $x$. The frames are ordered
lexicographically by (row reversal, column reversal, transposition), false first, so every frame
without transposition is followed by the transposed frame at the same corner.
Table~\ref{tab:catalogue} lists the entries. In the table, $a$ is either colour and $\bar a$ the other, and ``empty'' means that a cell holds no endpoint.
\ent{L2}--\ent{L5}, \ent{E1}, \ent{B}, \ent{C} and \ent{D} are \emph{frame entries}, which fire
when they match in one of the $8$ frames.

\paragraph{Global group (\ent{P}, \ent{T1}, \ent{T2}).}
\ent{P} is excluded by a sign count, \ent{T1} and \ent{T2} by planarity.

% lean: ZZN/Parity.lean:112 parity_of_solvable
% lean: ZZN/Parity.lean:157 parityOk_of_solvable
\begin{lemma}[\ent{P}]\label{lem:parity}
In a solution of an instance of any size, $\sum\sigma$ over the four endpoints equals
$2\cdot(wh\bmod 2)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Along a path the signs alternate, so twice the sum of $\sigma$ over a path equals the sum of
$\sigma$ over its two ends. A solution covers each cell exactly once, and the sum of $\sigma$ over
a $w\times h$ grid is $wh\bmod 2$. Adding the two paths gives the claim.
\end{proof}

% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:31 Closed
% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:42 ray
% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:48 par
% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:137 parP
% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:140 faces_around
% lean: ZZN/Planar.lean:184 parP_chain (uses :61 par_down, :96 par_right, :162 parP_adj)
The planarity arguments treat cells as points of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and use one separation fact. Let
$\Gamma$ be a closed walk of unit steps. The \emph{crossing parity} of a point not on $\Gamma$ is
the parity of the number of steps of $\Gamma$ that an upward ray from the centre of a unit square
with a corner at the point crosses, the same for all four such squares. All points of a chain of
unit steps that avoids $\Gamma$ have the same crossing parity. Two unit squares that share an edge
differ in crossing parity by the number of steps of $\Gamma$ on that edge, modulo $2$
(supplement, section ``Proof of the separation fact'').

% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:411 t1_core (hAin, hQin: both chains lie in the grid)
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:538 t1_sound
\begin{lemma}[\ent{T1}]\label{lem:t1}
On a grid with both sides at least $2$, two disjoint chains of cells of the grid whose four ends
lie on the border do not have their ends alternate in clockwise order. So \ent{T1} never fires on a
well-formed solvable instance with both sides at least $2$.
\end{lemma}
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:67 psi
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:93 anc
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:200 par_face
% lean: ZZN/SoundT1.lean:391 face_parP
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Close one chain $A$ into a closed walk $\Gamma$ by a connector that runs clockwise through the
points just outside the grid, from the point next to one end of $A$ to the point next to the other
(Figure~\ref{fig:sep}, left). A border cell not on $A$ has crossing parity $1$ exactly when the
connector passes a point next to it. When the ends alternate, this holds for exactly one end of the
other chain, although that chain avoids $\Gamma$, which contradicts the separation fact.
\end{proof}

% lean: ZZN/SoundT2.lean:122 t2_core
% lean: ZZN/SoundT2.lean:334 t2_sound
\begin{lemma}[\ent{T2}]\label{lem:t2}
\ent{T2} never fires on a well-formed solvable instance.
\end{lemma}
% lean: ZZN/SoundT2.lean:26 dpath
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Let $(r,c)$ and $(r+1,c+1)$ be the endpoints of one colour and $A$ its solution path. In
coordinates $(2x,2y)$, $A$ with the midpoints of its steps is closed into a walk $\Gamma$ through
the midpoint of $(r+1,c),(r+1,c+1)$, the centre of the square and the midpoint of $(r,c),(r,c+1)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:sep}, right). The other path with its midpoints avoids
$\Gamma$, but exactly one step of $\Gamma$, at the centre, lies between its ends $(r,c+1)$ and
$(r+1,c)$, so their crossing parities differ.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Forced-route group (\ent{R}).}
Some placements of endpoints at a corner force the first cells of their paths. \ent{R} is
\ent{T1} after these forced routes. Three rules (Figure~\ref{fig:forced}) move endpoints to the
ends of their forced routes and mark the cells that the routes fill as \emph{settled}. The paths
between the moved endpoints avoid the settled cells (Lemma~\ref{lem:r}), and \ent{R} fires when
the moved endpoints alternate in colour along a walk around the border that bypasses these cells.

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:257 Rewrite
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:260 rewritesAt
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:482 forced_r1
\begin{itemize}
\item \ent{R1}, corner turn. $(0,1)$ holds an endpoint and $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$ are empty. In a
  solution the corner cell has its two neighbours as path neighbours, so the path of the endpoint
  starts $(0,1)$, $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$. Move the endpoint to $(1,0)$ and settle $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$.
\item \ent{R2}, diagonal pair. $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ hold endpoints of one colour and $(0,1)$,
  $(1,0)$, $(0,2)$, $(2,0)$ are empty. Move $(0,0)$ to $(0,2)$ and $(1,1)$ to $(2,0)$, and settle
  $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$.
\item \ent{R3}, staircase pair. $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ hold endpoints of different colours and the
  cells $(i,0)$, $(0,i)$ for $i\le 3$ and $(1,1)$ are empty. Move $(1,2)$ to $(0,3)$ and $(2,1)$
  to $(3,0)$, and settle $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(0,2)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(2,0)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$.
\end{itemize}
\ent{R1}, \ent{R2}, \ent{R3} also need at least $2$, $3$, $4$ frame rows, and \ent{R2}, \ent{R3} at least $3$,
$4$ frame columns.
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:285 rwStep
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:294 rewriteAll
The \emph{effective endpoints} start as the endpoints. The frames are visited in their order, and
in each frame only the first rule that matches the effective endpoints, in the order \ent{R3},
\ent{R2}, \ent{R1}, is tried. It is applied unless it would settle a settled cell or move an
endpoint onto one, in which case the frame is skipped. In the rules, a cell is empty when it holds no
effective endpoint, settled or not.

% lean: ZZN/PassTR.lean:1104 shapes
% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:366 effAlt3
The \emph{bypass walk} follows the border in clockwise order, except that at a corner with
settled cells it leaves the border and passes through the corner's \emph{staircase}, the cells
that are not settled and share an edge or a corner with a settled cell. On a wide instance
the settled cells at a corner, its \emph{shape}, are none or the cells settled by \ent{R1}, by
\ent{R1} transposed, by \ent{R2} or by \ent{R3}, so the walk is well defined. \ent{R} fires when at least one rule was
applied, no effective endpoint is settled, the four effective endpoints are distinct and each
occurs once on the bypass walk, and their colours alternate along it
(Figure~\ref{fig:forced}(d)).

\begin{lemma}[\ent{R}]\label{lem:forced}\label{lem:r}
In a solution of a wide instance with no effective endpoint settled, the path of each colour
whose two effective endpoints are distinct contains a run of consecutive cells that joins them
and contains no settled cell. So \ent{R} never fires on a solvable wide instance.
\end{lemma}
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:482 forced_r1
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:496 forced_r2
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:608 forced_r3
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:1711 exitTab
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:1733 safeB
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:1741 exit_facts
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:1749 exit_order
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:2484 corner_far
% lean: ZZN/SoundR.lean:2492 exits_disj
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
At each endpoint the path starts with a forced part through settled cells that ends at an
effective endpoint, and every settled cell lies on such a part, so the run between the forced
parts at the two ends of a path avoids the settled cells. \ent{R1} states its forced part. For
\ent{R2}, if $(0,1)$ is the path neighbour of $(0,0)$, then $(1,0)$ has the path
neighbours $(1,1)$ and $(2,0)$, so the forced parts are $(0,0),(0,1),(0,2)$ and $(1,1),(1,0),(2,0)$.
The other case is the transpose, in which the parts end at the two effective endpoints in
exchanged order. For \ent{R3} the forced parts are $(1,2),(0,2),(0,3)$ and
$(2,1),(1,1),(0,1),(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0)$, or their transposes.

When \ent{R} fires, this gives two disjoint runs between the effective endpoints that avoid the
settled cells. In the \emph{doubled grid}, of sides $2w-1$ and $2h-1$, in which $(2x,2y)$ stands
for the cell $(x,y)$, each run with the midpoints of its steps is a chain. A finite table, with
one entry per shape and staircase cell, extends it from each end to the border through settled
cells, midpoints of steps at a settled cell or at that end, and points between four cells. A kernel-checked computation
shows that the extensions of distinct staircase cells are disjoint, avoid every point the other
run can use, and reach the border in the order of the bypass walk. So the extended chains are
disjoint with alternating ends on the border, which contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:t1}.
\end{proof}

through an \emph{exit}, a step from a window cell $(i,5)$ to $(i,6)$ or from $(5,j)$ to $(6,j)$. The $12$ exits are numbered down the right side and then leftward along the
bottom. A \emph{boundary state}, a state after the last cell, lists the pieces by their two ends,
each an endpoint or an exit, and the colours completed inside. Its \emph{outside ends} are the
\emph{used} exits, those at which a piece ends, and $F$ for \ent{B}.

A boundary state satisfies the \emph{completion test} when all of the following hold.
\begin{itemize}
\item The number of outside ends is even and not zero.
\item The sum of $\sigma$ over the cells just outside the used exits, plus $\sigma(F)$ for
  \ent{B}, is $2\cdot(wh\bmod 2)$.
\item The outside ends, in exit order with $F$ last, have a non-crossing pairing with the
  following properties. Following the pairing and the inside pieces alternately from an endpoint
  gives one path per colour not completed inside. Every outside end lies on one of these paths.
  If $F$ lies just outside a used exit, it is paired with that exit.
\end{itemize}
A certificate states that no boundary state satisfies the test.

\begin{lemma}[window certificates]\label{lem:window}
If a wide instance matches, in the identity frame, a configuration of the window group whose cells
lie in the window (for \ent{B}, all but $F$) and has a solution, then the boundary state of the
solution restricted to the window satisfies the completion test. So a certificate shows that every
such instance is unsolvable.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]

\end{proof}

\subsection{Deleting and inserting free lines}\label{sec:delete}

The converse of Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} is an induction on the area, whose step deletes two
adjacent free lines, under conditions that keep the instance passing, and inserts them back by
band extension.

A \emph{pair deletion} deletes two free lines $x=d$ and $x=d+1$ of an axis of length $w\ge 13$, so
that the side stays at least $11$, in one of three kinds (Figure~\ref{fig:deletions}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Edge strip.} $d=0$ and lines $0$ to $3$ are free, or $d+2=w$ and the last four are free.
\item \emph{Wide-gap compression.} Lines $d$, $d+1$ are among $10$ consecutive free lines, with an
  endpoint on a line before these $10$ and an endpoint on a line after them.
\item \emph{Distant-endpoint compression.} $5\le d$, $d+7\le w$, lines $d$, $d+1$ and one of $d-1$,
  $d+2$ are free, and there are endpoints with $x<d$ and with $x>d+1$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{lemma}[band extension]\label{lem:band}\label{lem:lift}
Let $I$ be a solvable instance, well-formed or not, with $r<w$ and the line $x=r$ free. Then the
instance obtained by inserting two lines after $x=r$ is solvable, and the construction in the
proof turns every solution of $I$ into a solution of it. So an instance $J$ is solvable when a
pair deletion of $J$ gives a solvable instance, and the construction turns every solution of
that instance into one of $J$.
\end{lemma}

% lean: ZZN/Statement.lean:399 absorbE

% lean: ZZN/Reductions.lean:41 lift_delete
% lean: ZZN/Reductions.lean:74 lift_delete'
\begin{proof}
Let $p,q$ be a solution of $I$. A column $y$ is a \emph{crossing} when a path steps between
$(r,y)$ and $(r+1,y)$, and \emph{non-crossing} otherwise. Move the cells with $x>r$ to $x+2$,
which leaves a \emph{band} of two new lines $x=r+1$ and $x=r+2$, and \emph{stretch} each step at a crossing through
the two new cells of its column, which gives the column a \emph{middle step} between them. A
\emph{detour} covers a non-crossing column $y$ from a neighbouring column $y'$ with a middle step,
by replacing that step by the run $(r+1,y')$, $(r+1,y)$, $(r+2,y)$, $(r+2,y')$. Column $y$ gets a
middle step and $y'$ loses its own, so the construction starts from two neighbouring columns with
middle steps, one for the columns to their left and one for those to their right, or from one
such column at an edge of the grid. A \emph{gap seed}, a position between two neighbouring
crossings or between an edge of the grid and a crossing next to it, has them. A \emph{step seed},
a step of a path along $x=r$ between neighbouring non-crossing columns $y$, $y+1$, gets them when
it is replaced by the run $(r,y)$, $(r+1,y)$, $(r+2,y)$, $(r+2,y+1)$, $(r+1,y+1)$, $(r,y+1)$. From
the leftmost seed, the non-crossing columns to its left are covered in order away from the seed by detours from their
right neighbour, and those to its right from their left neighbour (Figure~\ref{fig:band}). Each
replacement swaps a step for a run with the same two ends that adds only uncovered new cells, and
every new cell gets covered, so the result is a solution.

A seed exists. Otherwise columns $0$ and $h-1$ are non-crossing, no two neighbouring columns are
both crossings, and no step along $x=r$ joins two neighbouring non-crossing columns. Let $y$ be
non-crossing. The cell $(r,y)$ is no endpoint and has no path neighbour at $(r+1,y)$, so one of
its two path neighbours lies on $x=r$, in a neighbouring column $c$, which is a crossing. The
cell $(r,c)$ has the path neighbour $(r+1,c)$ and so at most one on $x=r$. So $y\mapsto c$ is
injective, and so is $c\mapsto c-1$ from the crossings to the non-crossing columns other than
$h-1$. The non-crossing columns are then at most as many as the crossings and at least one more,
a contradiction.

For the last statement, every kind of pair deletion of lines $x=d$, $x=d+1$ leaves a free line
$x=d+2$ or $x=d-1$ next to the pair. Inserting two lines after it in the smaller instance (after
its line $x=d$ in the first case, after $x=d-1$ in the second) gives back $J$. When both lines are
free the lift uses $x=d+2$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Cut trees.}\phantomsection\label{lem:cut} The grid certificates solve an instance by
recursive splits, recorded as a cut tree, or, for some instances, by explicit paths. A \emph{cut
tree} is read top-down against a rectangle and a list of \emph{terminal pairs}, pairs of cells, possibly
equal, to be joined by disjoint paths that cover the rectangle. The root is read against the grid with the
two endpoint pairs. A leaf records nothing. A cut node records an axis, a position at which it cuts
its rectangle between two adjacent lines into two parts, and for each terminal pair the part of its
first end and one of three types (Figure~\ref{fig:cut}(a) shows the second).
\begin{itemize}
\item The pair stays in one part, which gets it.
\item The path crosses the cut at a position $i$. Each part gets the pair of one old end and its
  cell next to the cut at $i$.
\item Both ends lie in one part, and the path leaves it at position $i$ and returns at $j$. That
  part gets the pairs (first end, its cell at $i$) and (its cell at $j$, second end), and the other
  part gets the pair of its cells at $i$ and $j$.
\end{itemize}
A leaf is \emph{valid} when its rectangle has one pair, either a single cell that is the whole
$1\times 1$ rectangle or an IPS-admissible pair of the rectangle. A tree \emph{checks} when every
cut lies strictly inside its rectangle, every cut node gives a type to each of its pairs, and every
leaf is valid. By induction on the tree, a tree that checks gives disjoint paths that join the
pairs and cover the rectangle. A valid leaf is covered by its cell or by a Hamiltonian path, and at a cut the paths of the two
parts are joined across the two adjacent cells at each crossing position.

crossing position is an anchor plus a fixed integer offset, with a negative result taken as $0$.
The anchor is $0$, the side length, or one of the four endpoint coordinates, on the axis along
which the position is measured. A template \emph{checks} on an instance when the tree obtained by
evaluating its positions there checks, and the instance is then solvable. One template solves many
instances (Figure~\ref{fig:template}(b) and~(c)).

\emph{row tuple} of an instance lists the $x$-coordinates of $s_0,t_0,s_1,t_1$, and the
\emph{column tuple} their $y$-coordinates. For $w\le h$, the \emph{canonical image} of an instance
is its image, under the symmetries without transposition, or all symmetries when $w=h$, with the
lexicographically least row tuple and, among those, the least column tuple.

\begin{lemma}[small passing instances]\label{lem:small}\label{lem:boxes}
A passing wide instance with both sides at most $16$, on which no pair deletion applies on either
axis, is solvable.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $I$ be such an instance, with $w\le h$ after transposing if needed, which keeps it wide and
passing (Lemma~\ref{lem:pass}) and maps solutions to solutions. Let $J$ be the canonical image of
$I$. Every symmetry keeps the absence of pair deletions, so $J$ admits none.

For each of the $21$ sizes $11\le w\le h\le 16$ the kernel checks a grid certificate (supplement,
section ``Certificate formats and the programs that found them''). The certificates list
$16\,477$ row tuples in all, among them the row tuple of every canonical image that admits no pair
deletion on the $x$-axis, and for each row tuple templates with symmetries, failing column tuples
and explicit paths. The check shows that every well-formed canonical image of such a size that
admits no pair deletion on either axis, and on which \ent{P} does not fire, has a template that
checks on its image under a recorded symmetry, or recorded explicit paths that solve it, or a
failing record. A failing record names, for the instance and for each of its three reflections, in
$x$, in $y$ and in both, an entry that fires on it. When \ent{P} fires on an instance, it fires on
its three reflections.

So $J$ is solvable, or neither $J$ nor any of its three reflections passes. The instance $I$ is an
image of $J$ and passes, and on wide instances passing is kept by
transposition (Lemma~\ref{lem:pass}) and by the exchanges, since no entry depends on the order of
the two endpoints of a colour or on which colour is $0$ (checked entry by entry in the Lean
development). So $J$ or one of its three reflections passes, which is why a failing record covers
all four. Hence $J$ is solvable, and the inverse symmetry maps a solution of $J$ to one of $I$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:char}]
Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} gives one direction. For the other, let $I$ be a passing wide instance and
argue by induction on the area. If a pair deletion applies on the $x$-axis, the smaller instance is
wide and passes (Lemma~\ref{lem:pass}), so it is solvable, and Lemma~\ref{lem:lift} gives a
solution of $I$. A pair deletion on the $y$-axis is handled on the transpose, which is wide and
passes (Lemma~\ref{lem:pass}). Otherwise both sides are at most $16$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:wide}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:small} applies.
\end{proof}

Deciding (Algorithm~\ref{alg:decide}) applies the catalogue or the plug DP. Constructing
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:construct}) follows the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:char}. It deletes free lines
while it can. The instance that remains is the \emph{final instance}. SolveDirect solves it by the
plug DP with paths or by a lookup in the grid certificates, and the solution is lifted back through
the deletions. Each lift is a pass over both paths, and deleting one pair at a time needs
a number of lifts that grows with $M=\max(w,h)$. BlockLayer therefore first deletes \emph{blocks}, each
of many free lines. On a wide instance it deletes at most $36$ blocks, after which both sides are at
most $37$ (proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:steps}), and PairLayer deletes the remaining pairs, each time
at the least $d$ among $0,\ldots,w-2$ at which a pair deletion applies. The
\emph{transpose} of a pair of paths exchanges the two coordinates of every cell. SelectBlock, the
lift of a block and TableLookup are defined after the algorithms. The supplement has pseudocode for
them (section ``Block selection, block lifts and table lookup'') and the recursion that builds the
Hamiltonian paths of the leaves (section ``Building a Hamiltonian path in a rectangle'').

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Construct$(I)$, returning a solution or none}\label{alg:construct}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Statex
\Function{BlockLayer}{$I$}
  \If{a side of $I$ is at most $10$} \Return \Call{PairLayer}{$I$} \EndIf
  \State $J\gets I$ if SelectBlock returns a block for $I$, else the transpose of $I$
  \State $\beta\gets$ SelectBlock$(J)$
  \If{$\beta$ is none} \Return \Call{PairLayer}{$I$} \EndIf
  \State $S\gets$ \Call{BlockLayer}{$J$ with $\beta$ deleted}
  \If{$S$ is none} \Return none \EndIf
  \State $S'\gets$ the lift of $\beta$ applied to $S$, a solution of $J$
  \State \Return $S'$ if $J=I$, else the transpose of $S'$
\EndFunction
\Function{PairLayer}{$I$}
  \If{a side of $I$ is at most $10$} \Return \Call{SolveDirect}{$I$} \EndIf
  \State $J\gets I$ if a pair deletion applies on the $x$-axis of $I$, else the transpose of $I$
  \State $d\gets$ the least $d\ge 0$ at which a pair deletion of any kind applies on the $x$-axis of $J$
  \If{there is none} \Return \Call{SolveDirect}{$I$} \EndIf
  \State $S\gets$ \Call{PairLayer}{$J$ with the lines $x=d$, $x=d+1$ deleted}
  \If{$S$ is none} \Return none \EndIf
  \State $S'\gets$ $S$ lifted as in Lemma~\ref{lem:lift}, a solution of $J$
  \State \Return $S'$ if $J=I$, else the transpose of $S'$
\EndFunction
\Function{SolveDirect}{$I$}
  \State $S\gets$ the plug DP with paths on $I$ if a side of $I$ is at most $10$, else
    TableLookup$(I)$\label{line:final}
  \State \Return $S$ if $S$ is a solution of $I$, else none
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Blocks.} A \emph{block} is a run of $2k\ge 2$ free lines of one axis, deleted at once.
Deleting the block of lines $x=c,\ldots,c+2k-1$ lowers $w$ by $2k$ and lowers by $2k$ the
$x$-coordinate of every endpoint with $x\ge c+2k$. SelectBlock chooses a block on the $x$-axis of a
wide instance. BlockLayer calls it only on wide instances, since it reaches SelectBlock only with
both sides at least $11$, and deleting free lines and transposing keep an instance well-formed. Let $x_{\min}$ and $x_{\max}$ be the least and the greatest $x$-coordinate of
an endpoint. A block \emph{admits} $k$ when $k$ satisfies the condition of that block and $2k+11\le w$. The last
inequality keeps the side at least $11$. SelectBlock tries the top block, then the bottom block,
then the middle blocks, takes the first that admits some $k\ge 1$, and uses the largest $k$ that it
admits. The \emph{top block},
lines $0$ to $2k-1$, leaves at least $2$ free lines before the line $x=x_{\min}$, so its condition
is $2k+2\le x_{\min}$. The \emph{bottom block}, the last $2k$ lines, leaves at least $2$ free lines
after the line $x=x_{\max}$, so its condition is $x_{\max}+2k+3\le w$. A \emph{middle block}, lines
$a+2$ to $a+1+2k$, lies between endpoint lines $x=a$ and $x=b$ with $a<b$ and no endpoint on a line
strictly between them, and leaves at least $8$ free lines between them, so its condition is
$a+2k+9\le b$. Every other pair $(a,b)$ admits no $k$. The pairs $(a,b)$ are tried with $a$ running over the $x$-coordinates of
$s_0,t_0,s_1,t_1$ in this order and, for each $a$, $b$ running over them in the same order, and the
first pair that admits some $k\ge 1$ is taken. If no case admits a $k$, SelectBlock returns none. A returned block is given by its kind, its $k$
and, for a middle block, $a$.

A \emph{snake} over a list of columns runs along the list on the first new line, along the reversed
list on the second, and so on through all $2k$ new lines, taken in increasing order of $x$. A step
is \emph{along} a line when both its cells are on the line. \emph{EdgeSplice} puts back
a bottom block into a solution $p,q$ of the smaller instance. Let $n=w-2k$ be the number of lines of
the smaller instance, so that the new lines are $x=n,\ldots,w-1$. EdgeSplice takes the first step in
path order of $p$ along the line $x=n-1$, or of $q$ if $p$ has none, between columns $y$ and $y+1$.
Between the two cells of the step it inserts the snake over $y,y-1,\ldots,0$ followed by the
reverse of the snake over $y+1,\ldots,h-1$, and it reverses the inserted run as a whole when the
step goes from column $y+1$ to column $y$. Such a step exists when $h\ge 5$, since the line $x=n-1$
then has a cell that is not an endpoint, and at most one of the two path neighbours of that cell
lies off the line. If neither path has such a step, EdgeSplice returns $p,q$ unchanged.

\emph{MiddleSplice} puts back a middle block after the free line $x=r$, where $r=a+1$, as band
extension does (Lemma~\ref{lem:band}) from the leftmost seed, with the $2k$ new lines
$x=r+1,\ldots,r+2k$ in place of two (Figure~\ref{fig:blocks}). The cells with $x>r$ move to $x+2k$.
Let the leftmost seed lie between columns $R-1$ and $R$, with $R=0$ when it lies before column $0$
and $R=h$ when it lies after column $h-1$. The \emph{group} of a column $y$ is $y$
followed by the non-crossing columns next to it going away from the seed, $y-1,y-2,\ldots$ when
$y<R$ and $y+1,y+2,\ldots$ otherwise, up to the next crossing column or the edge of the grid. A crossing step in column $y$ gets, between its two cells,
the snake over the group of $y$, reversed when the step goes toward smaller $x$. If the leftmost seed
is a step seed, its step gets the snake over the group of $R-1$ followed by the reverse of the snake
over the group of $R$, the whole reversed when the step goes from $R$ to $R-1$. A gap seed needs no
replacement of its own. The \emph{lift} of a bottom block is EdgeSplice.
The lift of a top block reflects the solution by $x\mapsto n-1-x$, applies EdgeSplice and reflects
the result by $x\mapsto w-1-x$. The lift of a middle block is MiddleSplice.

\begin{lemma}[blocks]\label{lem:blocks}
If SelectBlock returns a block for a wide instance $I$, deleting it is a sequence of pair
deletions, and its lift turns every solution of the smaller instance into a solution of $I$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the free lines that remain and the side of at least $11$, deleting a top or bottom block is a
sequence of edge strips, and deleting a middle block a sequence of wide-gap compressions. Each
splice replaces steps by runs through new cells with the same two ends, and the snakes pass
through every new cell once.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[construction]\label{thm:construct}
Whenever Construct returns paths, they are a solution of its input, for every input. Construct
returns a solution for every well-formed solvable instance. Both hold for both variants of the final
solver.
\end{theorem}

explicit paths, since the final instance passes (Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows a run).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[step bounds]\label{thm:steps}
Let $M=\max(w,h)$. On every instance,
\begin{enumerate}
\item the counted Decide takes at most $(K(10)+300\,000)(wh+w+h+1)$ steps, and
\item the counted Construct takes at most $37\,(80(M+1)^2+400) + (10M+1)\,K(10) + 3(10M+1)^2 + 10$
  steps plus a constant, $B_L = 74\cdot 700\cdot 38^2 + 371\,K(10) + 28\,000\,000 + 3\cdot 593^2 +
  10$ for the table variant and $B_R = 74\cdot 700\cdot 38^2 + 593\,K(16) + 3\cdot 593^2 + 10$ for
  the plug-DP variant.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The inequalities are proved in the Lean development. The reasons are as follows. In (1) the
counted Decide runs one of two branches. The catalogue takes at most $300\,000\,(w+h+1)$ steps, and
the plug DP, run only when a side is at most $10$, at most $(wh+1)\,K(10)$. The stated bound is at
least each of the two. In (2), the terms with
$10M+1$ bound the plug DP and the check of its answer on a thin input, of area at most $10M$. On a
wide input, the \emph{open gaps} of an axis are the top block, the bottom block and the $16$ ordered
pairs $(a,b)$ of endpoint coordinates, each counted when it admits some $k\ge 1$. The two axes have at most $2\,(1+1+16)=36$ open gaps, and deleting a block lowers their
number, so BlockLayer is called at most $37$ times, at most $80(M+1)^2+400$ steps each. An axis
of length $n\ge 13$ that admits no block has $x_{\min}\le 3$, $x_{\max}\ge n-4$ and consecutive
endpoint lines at most $10$ apart, so $n\le 3+3\cdot 10+4=37$. PairLayer then makes at most $w+h\le 74$
calls at $700\cdot 38^2$ steps each. $K$ is nondecreasing. In $B_L$ the sum
$371\,K(10)+28\,000\,000$ bounds the final solver on every instance with sides at most $37$, the
first term for the plug DP when a side is at most $10$, on an area of at most $10\cdot 37$, and the
second for TableLookup otherwise. In $B_R$ the term $593\,K(16)$ bounds the plug DP on a final
instance, which has a side at most $16$ and the other at most $37$. The term $3\cdot 593^2+10$
bounds the check of the result (supplement, section ``The counted model and the step accounting'', subsection
``Construction'').
\end{proof}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

% lean: FinalN.lean:43 passes_iff_solvable_K

A wide instance of two-colour Zig-Zag Numberlink is solvable if and only if no entry of a finite
catalogue of forbidden patterns fires on it. Every well-formed instance is decided within $O(wh)$
counted steps and solved, when solvable, within $O(M^2)$ counted steps, $M=\max(w,h)$, all proved in Lean~4. New
are the characterization on rectangles with the endpoints anywhere, the conditions under which a
pair deletion keeps a passing instance passing, the $O(wh)$ decision procedure, the $O(M^2)$
construction, and the Lean proof.

The reduction by deleting and inserting two lines is known for one path~\cite{ips82} and for two
paths on a torus~\cite{makino06}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The constants are large. The bound on the counted steps per cell of the
plug DP at frontier width $10$ is $K(10)\approx 9.45\cdot 10^{8}$. Sufficiency on sides $11$ to
$16$, the pair deletion on an axis of length $17$ to $22$ and the necessity of \ent{B}, \ent{C}
and \ent{D} rest on kernel-checked per-case computations, and the paper gives no uniform argument for these cases. The catalogue is not
minimal. On a wide instance with $wh$ even, the configurations marked $*$ and $\dagger$ in
Table~\ref{tab:lists} make \ent{R} fire whenever they match. The step model counts list
operations, as set out in Section~\ref{sec:algo}, and makes arithmetic on natural numbers of any
size free.

\paragraph{Not shown.} The paper gives no forbidden-pattern condition for instances with a side at
most $10$, which the plug DP decides, and does not determine whether the bound $11$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:char} can be lowered. It does not say how the lists of Table~\ref{tab:lists} and
the rules \ent{R1} to \ent{R3} were found. It gives no decision procedure polynomial in the bit
length of the input and no construction linear in the output size $wh$. It does not treat more
than two pairs or grids other than rectangles.
